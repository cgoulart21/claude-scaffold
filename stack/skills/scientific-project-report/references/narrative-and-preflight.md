# Narrative derivation and pre-flight

Use this reference after the four control views are stable enough to expose the
project’s contradictions and open gates, or to record that no material contradiction
or configuration mismatch was found.

## Derive, do not merely retell

The report’s organizing sequence should follow the scientific argument:

1. What question or decision motivated the work?
2. What system, objects, and configurations were actually studied?
3. Which theory supports the chosen measurements, models, and interpretations?
4. Which methods generated the evidence that remains current?
5. What did each evidence class establish?
6. How do the results answer the question, and under which limits?
7. Which claims remain blocked, and what evidence would unlock them?

Chronology is supporting structure. Use it in the main narrative only when a correction,
negative result, or design transition is part of the scientific explanation.

## Default section logic

Adapt headings to the target field rather than forcing a house style.

- **Introduction:** problem, external context, research question, objectives, and the
  intended contribution. Do not preview claims more strongly than the evidence matrix.
- **System or study object:** identify the physical, numerical, and digital objects that
  a reader must distinguish.
- **Theoretical foundation:** include concepts that justify a method, equation,
  parameter, expected mechanism, interpretation, or limitation. Avoid detached textbook
  exposition.
- **Materials and methods:** describe the defensible sequence that produced current
  evidence. Put historically important diagnostic work here only when it is itself a
  method or explains a correction.
- **Results:** group by scientific question or evidence chain. Label reproduced
  calculations, simulations, digital checks, observations, and measurements explicitly.
- **Discussion:** compare evidence, explain mechanisms, analyze sensitivity and
  identifiability, relate findings to literature, and state alternative explanations.
  Do not introduce unsupported numerical results.
- **Conclusions:** answer the objectives with the narrowest accurate wording and expose
  consequential open gates.
- **Abstract:** write last from the verified body; include conditions for any headline
  prediction and distinguish measured from modeled outcomes.

## Paragraph pattern

For a material result, prefer a four-part paragraph:

1. **Claim:** the bounded statement being made.
2. **Evidence:** what source, object, configuration, and method support it.
3. **Interpretation:** why the result matters or what mechanism explains it.
4. **Limit:** what assumption, uncertainty, countercheck, or open gate bounds it.

Not every sentence needs all four parts, but every consequential claim should make them
recoverable nearby.

## Evidence-aware language

Choose verbs that preserve the evidence class:

- theory **implies** or **predicts** within stated assumptions;
- literature **reports** a result for its own system;
- a reproduced computation **returns** or the model **predicts** a value;
- tests **verify the implementation within the tested domain**;
- a digital check **closes the modeled geometry or rule**, not physical fabrication;
- a physical inspection **observes** a condition;
- a calibrated experiment **measures** a quantity on an identified specimen;
- an independent comparison **corroborates** or **fails to corroborate** a claim.

Reserve **validates**, **demonstrates**, and **establishes** for scopes in which the
necessary independent and physical evidence actually exists. When in doubt, state the
evidence directly instead of choosing a stronger verb.

## Attempt-inclusion test

For each abandoned or failed branch, ask:

- Does it explain the current choice?
- Does it reveal a transferable scientific limitation or confounder?
- Does it change the interpretation of a current result?
- Is it a meaningful negative result with a defined tested domain?
- Is it needed to reproduce the selected method?
- Would omitting it cause a reasonable reader to draw the wrong conclusion?

If all answers are no, omit it from the main report. If traceability still matters,
retain it in an appendix, source register, or project history rather than interrupting
the scientific argument.

## External-reader pass

Read the report as if none of the internal file names or project nicknames were known.
Check that:

- the research question and studied objects are clear before detailed methods;
- every acronym, symbol, revision label, and configuration is introduced;
- nominal and measured parameters are visibly different;
- the reader can tell which artifact or specimen each table and figure represents;
- methodological corrections are explained without requiring the reader to reconstruct
  the repository history;
- negative and null results have a stated domain rather than becoming universal claims;
- the next experimental or analytical step follows from the open-gates register.

## Claim-level pre-flight

For every material claim and quantitative result—including those in the body, abstract,
figures, tables, captions, discussion, conclusions, and recommendations—answer:

1. Which claim-matrix row authorizes this wording?
2. Which configuration-map row identifies the object?
3. Is the evidence primary, reproduced, derived, or merely narrative?
4. Which assumptions and operating conditions are necessary?
5. Is the numerical precision defensible?
6. Does a supersession, contradiction, or open gate narrow the claim?
7. Would changing “measured” to “predicted,” or “validated” to “verified within the
   model,” be more accurate?

If any answer is missing, repair the control views or narrow the prose before delivery.
